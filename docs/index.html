About me

I spent the fall 2025 semester on exchange at KAIST in Daejeon, South Korea, in the third year (fifth semester) of my bachelor’s line in Artificial Intelligence and Data at DTU. A line is DTU’s word for a degree programme.

KAIST was my second priority. My first was Caltech (California Institute of Technology), but as I remember it the only spot that year went to someone with a perfect GPA (grade point average), which is very unlikely on the AI and Data line. I picked KAIST because I love Korean food and culture, it’s fairly prestigious, and I wanted the challenge of a new study culture, new people and new places.

I had been to Korea once, as a tourist with two friends after high school in 2022. We saw Busan and a bit of Seoul, and I fell in love with the culture. I spoke no Korean and had never been to Daejeon. It’s small by Korean standards, but coming from Denmark it feels big, because the buildings are on a much larger scale, as if there were no height limit.

Words you’ll see

  • KAIST is the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology, about three hours by bus or train from Incheon International Airport near Seoul.
  • DTU is the Technical University of Denmark.
  • ISSS (International Scholar and Student Services) is KAIST’s office for international students, covering dorms, visa help, orientation and the buddy programme.
  • IRT (International Relations Team) is the KAIST office for admission papers and transcripts.
  • ECTS points (European Credit Transfer System) are the credit unit DTU uses, and a full semester is 30.
  • SU (Statens Uddannelsesstøtte) is the Danish state study grant.
  • NHI is Korean National Health Insurance, which enrols you automatically once you’re registered as a resident.
  • The residence card is the ID card every foreigner staying more than 90 days needs, still often called the ARC (Alien Registration Card).
  • KDS (kds.kaist.ac.kr) is the dorm website.
  • Beyond Borders is DTU’s exchange application portal.
  • merit.dtu.dk is DTU’s credit transfer system.
  • KTX (Korea Train eXpress) is the high-speed train.
  • Won (KRW) and kroner (DKK) are the Korean and Danish currencies. I use about 215 won to 1 krone (2025), so 10,000 won is about 47 kroner.
  • Building codes are a letter for the part of campus (W for west, N for north, E for east) plus a number. W6 is the exchange dorm, N11 is Kaimaru (the big food court), W2-1 is the International Center, and E15 is the main auditorium.

My scores

  1. KAIST overall10/10

    The first days are stressful, and after that it’s super fun

  2. Academics8/10

    My two favourite courses were excellent, but the exams test memorisation hard

  3. Social life10/10

    About 200 exchange students in my cohort, and I never felt lonely

  4. Dorm7/10

    Very cheap, with a gym and study space, but I shared a double room and there’s no kitchen

  5. Food10/10

    Kaimaru and cheap Korean barbecue, often all-you-can-eat

  6. Admin and bureaucracy2/10

    Paper forms everywhere, and getting the residence card was “one of the biggest messes ever”

  7. Value for money10/10

    No tuition, a dorm that cost me about 4,000 DKK for the whole semester, and cheap food

  8. Safety10/10

    I never felt unsafe

The admin score comes from the first week, when Korea, for all its technology, has you write the same information on three or four paper forms for one or two offices (see Arrival and first weeks).

Good fits

  • You like working with other people. My two favourite courses, CS454 AI-Based Software Engineering and CS473 Intro to Social Computing (CS is the School of Computing), were built on group work, and three of my five courses had oral presentations, which was great practice at pitching.
  • You want courses you can’t take at home. I’d use KAIST for “out-of-distribution” courses, borrowing the machine learning term for data unlike anything a model was trained on. Evolving code with evolutionary algorithms in CS454 was “a whole new dimension of thinking”.
  • You’re on a budget. Food is cheap enough that “you can live for almost no money”, the return flight at about 6,000 DKK was my biggest single expense, and I kept my SU and got four grants on top (see Money).
  • You can live with paperwork. Most of it comes in the first week, with a little more when you check out, so if you can give the forms a full day you’re through the worst of it.
  • You want a semester that can’t hurt your GPA. KAIST grades reach DTU only as “passed”, and for exchange students KAIST is “really hard to fail and really hard to stand out”.

Poor fits

  • You hate memorising. In CS360 Intro to Database we wrote SQL (Structured Query Language) on paper in the exam, graded on exact match, so “one mistake gives zero”.
  • You want to be taught everything in class. Lectures are 75 minutes and more superficial than at DTU, and you’re expected to prepare a lot on your own (see Academics).
  • Bureaucracy wrecks your mood. The 2 I gave admin is a real warning, even if most of the pain is over after the first week.
  • You’re a bachelor student who wants master’s courses. You normally can’t take graduate-level courses (see Applying).
  • You need a kitchen. The dorms have none, and ISSS’s shared International Kitchen had only 100 places in fall 2025.
  • You want a chill semester. Then I’d “probably choose somewhere else”, though it depends a lot on how all-in you want to go.

The full timeline

Dates move a little every year, so check DTU’s study abroad site (studyabroad.dtu.dk) and KAIST’s latest inbound fact sheet (on io.kaist.ac.kr) for your round. For a fall 2027 exchange, DTU’s deadline is 1 December 2026.
