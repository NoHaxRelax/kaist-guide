DTU’s selection rules

You apply through Beyond Borders. KAIST is a university-wide partner of DTU, open to BSc (Bachelor of Science), BEng (Bachelor of Engineering) and MSc (Master of Science) students for one semester, or two at most (DTU’s 2026 listing).

Selection is by grades only. DTU gives each spot to the eligible applicant with the highest GPA (grade point average) and works down the list. The motivational letter is optional, at most 2,000 characters, and only breaks a tie between applicants with exactly the same GPA. I wrote one anyway, but I wouldn’t lose sleep over it.

It’s supply and demand, so the cut-off moves every year. As I remember it, the lowest GPA admitted to KAIST in my year was between 9 and 9.5. DTU’s KAIST page in 2026 lists 10.0 as the realistic “min. expected GPA” and says KAIST can’t accept anyone below 8.4 on the Danish 12-point scale. In my view the competition isn’t completely even, because some study lines (DTU’s word for degree programmes) are easier than others in the first semesters, where your application GPA comes from.

With the application you hand in a GPA calculation from DTU’s Excel GPA calculator, your transcripts, language documentation if the partner university requires it, and a Joint Application Declaration if you apply together with friends.

To be eligible as a bachelor student (DTU’s 2025 to 2026 rules), you must be enrolled full time when you apply and throughout the stay, have at least 110 ECTS passed and two years of study behind you before departure, and have room in your programme to transfer at least 20 ECTS.

Round 1 closes on 1 December at 23:59, a month after the portal opens, and DTU replies within about three weeks. Round 2, for spots nobody took, closed on 1 February 2025 in my year, and people rejected in round 1 get preferential consideration there. If you accept a round 1 spot, you can’t drop it later for a round 2 spot.

Your five priorities

My five priorities were Caltech (California Institute of Technology), KAIST, the School of Engineering at Johns Hopkins University, NYU (New York University) and the University of Sydney, in that order. DTU turned down Caltech with “No more exchange spots” and granted KAIST, so the last three were never evaluated.

Have two or three backups you’d genuinely be happy with. I knew my grades would get me into KAIST, but that isn’t a given.

Advising sessions

DTU’s study abroad office runs short online advising sessions, booked through the booking page linked from DTU Study Abroad’s contact page. Pick “General Questions” for a study advisor, or the partnership manager for a region if you have questions about specific universities. I booked two for the same day in November 2024, one about the United States and Canada and one about Asia, 20 minutes each, and in the Asia one I asked how to find relevant courses at KAIST. You don’t need to be selected first, so book before the 1 December deadline.

The selection letter

Mine arrived on 16 December 2024. It shows how DTU evaluated each priority, names your DTU contact person and lists your conditions. Mine were to:

  • pass 110 ECTS at DTU before leaving
  • meet KAIST’s admission and language requirements
  • get the credit transfer pre-approved before departure
  • complete and transfer at least 20 ECTS
  • finish all six modules of the “Get ready for exchange” course on DTU Learn (DTU’s online learning platform)
  • have health, liability and accident insurance for the whole stay
  • apply for credit transfer and hand in a travel report when back

The last page is an official confirmation of exchange, which grant foundations want as proof, so keep it handy. After the letter, wait for DTU’s instructions before you start on KAIST’s side.

KAIST’s application

The KAIST application came months after the DTU one and was a bit stressful. For a fall exchange, DTU nominates you (tells KAIST officially that you’re its pick) by 1 April, KAIST sends a nomination code to your DTU coordinator (never to you, so ask for it), and you apply on KAIST’s portal by 15 April (KAIST’s fall 2025 and 2027 fact sheets, the PDFs it publishes for incoming exchange students). KAIST also wanted confirmation of the nomination from DTU.

Other exchange students have fallen into two traps on the portal (io.kaist.ac.kr/exchange.do). It needs a personal email address like Gmail, because university addresses don’t work. And only “Submit” counts, since “Temporarily Save” looks like submitting, and people have missed the deadline believing they had applied.

The fall 2025 fact sheet asks for official transcripts in English, an English test result unless you’re waived (see the next part), the photo page of your passport and an official 3 × 4 cm photo. KAIST also wants a GPA of at least 3.0 on its 4.3 scale and a year of full-time study. Extra paper copies are only for students from outside the OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development), so Danes can skip them, and the study plan is only a draft. Later you’ll need 3.5 × 4.5 cm photos for the student ID card and the visa (see Before you fly).

I submitted on 22 March 2025, and KAIST confirmed it the same day. The acceptance email came on 5 June, and on 23 June IRT emailed the welcome package with my Certificate of Admission and KAIST’s Certificate of Business Registration, which you both need for the visa (see Before you fly). KAIST asked me to check the certificate for errors, so read yours carefully.

There were moments when I doubted everything would line up, but it did. You can’t prepare for every curveball, so accept that you’re stepping into something you don’t know yet, and trust that “the person you’re becoming in the process” will catch you.

The English test

Check whether KAIST needs a test from you before you pay for one. DTU’s KAIST page (2026) lists no language test for DTU students, and KAIST’s fall 2025 fact sheet waives it for students from institutions that teach in English. I took the Duolingo English Test to be safe, at about 40 USD (US dollars) against about 200 for TOEFL (Test of English as a Foreign Language). The fact sheet names TOEFL iBT (the internet-based version) with 80, IELTS (International English Language Testing System) with 6.5, TOEIC (Test of English for International Communication) with 720, or a B2 (upper intermediate) certificate on the European language scale from the last two years. Duolingo isn’t on that list, so if you do need a test, check that KAIST accepts it before you book.

Course pre-approval

Before you leave, DTU has to pre-approve the KAIST courses you want credit for (forhåndsgodkendelse in Danish). DTU’s 2025 to 2026 guidance is to file on merit.dtu.dk 4 months before the stay and allow 8 weeks of processing. KAIST only publishes the real course list about 2 months before the semester, so you can pre-approve from the previous year’s list.

  1. Pick plausible courses early. While applying, browse KAIST’s public course catalogue (cais.kaist.ac.kr/totalOpeningCourse) and put a few courses that interest you in your DTU application too, since in my experience Beyond Borders expects to see that you’ve checked they’re relevant. You can change them later.
  2. Use an AI (artificial intelligence) tool for the first pass. Finding courses by hand took me 2 to 3 hours per university. KAIST’s catalogue downloads as a spreadsheet, which is easy to hand to Claude, Codex or a similar tool, but make sure you actually agree with its picks.
  3. File on merit.dtu.dk. There’s a step-by-step guide, and approval comes fairly quickly unless something is badly wrong.
  4. Re-file as often as you need. I filed 2 or 3 times, to cancel a course that didn’t run in fall (DTU updated it within 1 to 1.5 weeks), then to swap one and add another, and by late May 2025 I had six approved. Get backups approved too, since you won’t win every seat in registration. After you arrive, you just submit a complete new list.

Syllabi need a KAIST Portal ID, but you can email professors before you’re even admitted, and most of the six I asked about midterm dates in May 2025 answered within a few days. OTL (otl.kaist.ac.kr), a timetable and course review site built by KAIST students, also helps.

Only three of my six pre-approved courses made it onto my timetable, and I ended up with five (see Academics). DTU’s own credit transfer leaflet says hardly anyone ends up taking exactly the courses they had pre-approved.

Your Head of Studies (the person in charge of your programme at DTU) decides whether a KAIST course can cover a mandatory course group, while electives just have to be relevant for an engineer. On the AI and Data bachelor, exchange credits usually go to electives or programme-specific courses, and one course can be split across groups. I took a brain and cognitive sciences course because it could replace a course that’s normally mandatory at DTU. Questions go to merit@adm.dtu.dk.

You must transfer every pre-approved course you pass, and you must pass at least 20 ECTS per exchange semester, or DTU may reclaim grants it has paid you.

Credit conversion

A normal KAIST course is 3 credits (some 8-week courses are 1.5). DTU’s 2026 KAIST listing converts 32 KAIST credits to 60 ECTS for bachelor students, so one 3-credit course is worth 5.625 ECTS. As I understand it, DTU then converts the total in steps of 2.5 ECTS and rounds down.

KAIST’s 12-credit minimum only gives 22.5 ECTS, so reaching 30 takes about 16 KAIST credits, or 5 to 6 courses. Five 3-credit courses plus one 1.5-credit course come to 16.5 credits, which rounds down to 30 ECTS.

I passed all five of my courses and got 27.5 ECTS. I topped up with Society, Technology and Sustainability (5 ECTS) in DTU’s three-week January period, which brought the semester to 32.5.

Master’s students

Master’s students convert at a better rate. In DTU’s 2026 listing, 24 KAIST credits (500-level courses and up) make 60 ECTS for MSc students, so a 3-credit course is worth 7.5 ECTS and four courses make 30. KAIST expects graduate exchange students to take 3 to 4 courses (9 to 12 credits).

I’d heard master’s students get about double the ECTS per course, but the real difference is about a third, so 6 courses come to 45 ECTS. I also heard of someone who did most of their master’s coursework at KAIST and only had the thesis left back home, but DTU’s rules limit how far that goes. A DTU MSc is 120 ECTS, split into the polytechnical foundation (10), programme-specific courses (50), electives (30) and the thesis (30) (DTU’s programme structure). Courses transferred into a mandatory group must meet its learning objectives, which your Head of Studies judges course by course. You can count at most 10 ECTS of bachelor-level courses, only as electives, and you must pass at least 60 ECTS at DTU itself to get the diploma (DTU’s credit transfer rules). As I understand it, anything beyond the 30 ECTS of electives has to overlap closely with your programme’s own courses.

DTU and KAIST also run MSc dual degrees in Electrical Engineering, Engineering Acoustics, Mechanical Engineering and Offshore Wind Energy (2026 listing), with two consecutive semesters at KAIST and a degree from both universities.

KAIST course rules

  • At least half your courses must come from your host department. Mine was the School of Computing.
  • Bachelor exchange students take 100- to 400-level courses. To cross levels you need a course marked “Y” (mutually recognised), or, according to DTU, a professor’s signature in person on a paper add/drop form. The sources disagree on where graduate level starts (KAIST’s fall 2025 fact sheet says 400-level, DTU says 500-level), but I took three 400-level courses as a bachelor student without trouble.
  • Undergraduates must carry 12 to 24 KAIST credits a semester, which is 4 to 8 courses.
  • From spring 2025 KAIST added new course codes next to the old ones, so my course CS454 AI-Based Software Engineering showed up as CS40504 on my paper add/drop form, and it’s worth searching for both.
  • About 80 to 85% of undergraduate courses are taught in English, and one course can have both English and Korean sections, so check the language of each section.

Registration opens in mid-August with a lottery for full courses, and the dates are already in your acceptance letter, so put them in your calendar straight away (see Academics).
