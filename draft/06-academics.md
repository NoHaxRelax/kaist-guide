## Academics

I went as a third-year AI (artificial intelligence) and Data student, took five courses and passed all five. Four were in the School of Computing, whose course codes start with CS (computer science), and one was in Brain and Cognitive Sciences (BCS).

### My five courses

I've ranked them in a tier list, where S is the best, then A, B, C and D, and F is the worst.

| Course | Professor | My tier |
|---|---|---|
| CS454 AI-Based Software Engineering | Shin Yoo | S |
| CS473 Introduction to Social Computing | Joseph Seering | S |
| CS492(C) Diffusion and Flow Models | Minhyuk Sung | B |
| BCS 30041 Methods in Brain and Cognitive Sciences | Seung Woo Lee | C |
| CS360 Introduction to Database | Jae-Gil Lee | F |

**CS454 AI-Based Software Engineering (S).** The course is about evolutionary algorithms and evolving pieces of code, and it opened "a whole new dimension of thinking... you come out as a new engineer with a completely new tool in your toolbox." Next to graded assignments and quizzes, my team's project used a genetic algorithm to pick small, useful training sets out of a classic dataset of handwritten digits.

**CS473 Introduction to Social Computing (S).** You build your own social platform and learn what makes an app good and social. It's "code mixed with sociology mixed with internet culture", and Professor Joseph Seering "knew his shit, super impressive". Over five project stages with studio presentations, my team built MealMap, an app for international students to find and review food around KAIST, and we launched it in the exchange students' WhatsApp group. There were reading responses and no midterm, but the final exam was worth 30% of the grade.

**CS492(C) Diffusion and Flow Models (B).** This one ([diffusion.kaist.ac.kr](https://diffusion.kaist.ac.kr)) covers the maths and code behind image-generating models. In 2025 it had three programming assignments, two closed-book tests in class with two A4 cheat sheets allowed, two image-generation challenges in teams of three, guest lectures from Cornell Tech and Google, and online poster sessions at the end. I didn't do too well in it, but I still got a fine grade, and passing is what matters for the transfer.

**BCS 30041 Methods in Brain and Cognitive Sciences (C).** I took this to replace a course that's normally mandatory at DTU, so it was "more push than pull", but it turned out interesting. It's very niche, and most of it is memorising methods for monitoring and stimulating neural activity, with a midterm, a final, a term project and an individual presentation. I was the only English-speaking student, and the lectures sometimes switched to Korean.

**CS360 Introduction to Database (F).** The course is online apart from the exams, but you still have to "be present". Lectures run on Zoom with your webcam on while the professor reads the slides aloud for an hour and a half, which felt "almost dystopian". The paper exams are very memory-based. You write SQL (Structured Query Language, used to query databases) by hand, and one mistake gives zero on that answer. My notes from the semester say "Exam grading is based on exact match with the correct answer so do NOT do typos!!!" If your memory is below average, it's a bad fit. I'd take databases at DTU and use KAIST for what I call "out-of-distribution" courses, the ones that teach you something you wouldn't get at home.

### Registration and the lottery

The registration dates were in my acceptance letter in June, and ISSS sent the full rules on 5 August. In fall 2025 registration ran on [sugang.kaist.ac.kr](https://sugang.kaist.ac.kr) from 12:30 on Monday 11 August to 23:59 on Monday 18 August. Courses with more applicants than seats were settled by a random lottery on 12 August, with results out before 18:00, and the seats left over then went first come, first served. A seat you won in the lottery couldn't be cancelled until add/drop.

I forgot the lottery entirely, so I could only join courses that weren't full. I'm "living proof you can still get some pretty cool courses", but it cost me courses I wanted. Put the dates in your calendar the day your acceptance letter arrives, and check them for your year, because they can move. Go in with a ranked list and a backup for each course, ideally one that's already pre-approved. School of Computing courses are popular, and one fall 2025 exchange student reported losing every lottery for humanities courses.

If you already have a trip planned, ask the professors about midterm dates before you register. Ahead of my family trip to Rome in October (see Travel), I emailed six professors in May. Intro to Deep Learning, with more than 150 students, had a midterm date set by the portal that couldn't be moved, and three of my pre-approved courses dropped out of my plan, mainly over midterm clashes.

### Add/drop and withdrawal

In 2025 add/drop ran first come, first served from 09:30 on 25 August to 23:59 on 8 September. For a full course you can use the "Course Registration (Exceptions)" menu, which closed on 4 September that year, and say in the request that you're an exchange student. A professor can also sign you into a course on a paper form. Whatever you end up with, send DTU a complete new course list on merit.dtu.dk (see Applying).

After that you can still withdraw. In 2025 the window ran from 9 September to 17 October, and a withdrawn course shows as a W (withdrawn) on your KAIST transcript without counting toward your grade average. That's before midterm week (20 to 24 October), so you decide before you've sat a single exam. I withdrew from Foundation of Big Data Analytics in mid-September because of a timetable clash. The professor wasn't sure how it worked and sent me to the department, so ask the department directly.

### The CS454 rescue

In early September I dropped CS454 by accident while trying to drop a different course. I emailed Professor Shin Yoo, and he backed me on 8 September, the day add/drop closed. The Academic Registrar's Team, the registration office in E11, then granted an exception on the condition that I brought back the signed paper form the same day, and the course was back on my list that afternoon. For undergraduates it's form number 45 on [KAIST's academic forms page](https://www.kaist.ac.kr/en/html/edu/031007.html). Because I joined late, I also had to ask for an invite to the course's Slack, the team chat app it ran on. Both the registrar and the professor were quick to help once I asked politely.

### Teaching style

Lectures are 75 minutes long, in 90-minute timetable slots, and none of my courses had exercise sessions. At DTU you can show up, listen for two hours, do the exercises for two hours, and be caught up. At KAIST it's an hour and a quarter and then straight on to the next course, with tasks for next time, some of them mandatory, and whether you prepare is up to you. Overall I found the teaching more superficial than at DTU.

Most lectures are Monday to Thursday. Back-to-back classes look scary on the timetable, but lectures usually end 5 to 10 minutes early, and crossing campus takes about 10 minutes on foot, or much less by bike (see Campus life).

Check the language of each course section when you register (see Applying), and expect some Korean anyway. In a 2024 survey by the KAIST Herald, a campus newspaper, 84% of international students had seen professors switch to Korean in lectures meant to be English only.

Study for the exams throughout the semester, because midterms often count about as much as the final. Other exchange students report that the first two to three weeks are relaxed before the workload climbs sharply, and my December was crammed, with teaching right up to the exams and most project deadlines in the same few weeks. ### Attendance

Attendance counts toward your final grade. In CS473 it was 10% of the grade, and missing a presentation day also costs you points on that presentation. CS492(C) took attendance at the guest lectures and made both poster sessions mandatory, and even the online database course wanted your webcam on. Other exchange students report courses where you lose points from the very first absence.

### Grading and the curve

Most courses are graded on a bell curve, so your grade depends on where you rank in the class. The exams test memorisation hard, but if everyone does badly you're fine, and in an easy course a high score may be pulled down a bit. KAIST doesn't publish official curve percentages. Many English-taught courses also let students answer exams in Korean or English on the same curve, which a June 2026 column in the KAIST Herald argued puts international students at a disadvantage.

Even so, for exchange students it's "really hard to fail and really hard to stand out", and I heard of one student who got a truly awful score on a database exam and still passed. My theory, which is speculation I can't confirm, is that KAIST is lenient with exchange students because it helps its international ranking, so please don't try to exploit it. Don't stress about a bad midterm either, since a decent final usually gets you through. Your letter grades never reach DTU anyway (see Transcript and credits below), so unless you want to maximise, enjoy your stay.

### Professors and teaching assistants

Professors are flexible if you ask nicely, and I learned the "nicely" part the hard way. I was a bit direct with a TA (teaching assistant) in the diffusion course and got a reality check from the professor, who reminded me to be respectful to TAs, because they're giving their time to help you. After that I wrote what I wanted to say and had ChatGPT rephrase it politely ("say it the way I felt it, and it phrased it politely"), which worked really well.

In May the Brain course professor had told me that coming back from Rome on 20 October wouldn't clash with the midterm on the 21st. But I landed at Incheon, Seoul's main airport, that afternoon and got back to Daejeon exhausted, so I asked to move the midterm's weight onto the final. The professor agreed to base my grade on the final, warning me that this adds variance, and I accepted because the material was easy.

When the same course's announcement and lecture slides gave different deadlines for the term project topic, I went by the later one, explained myself to the TA, and there was no deduction.

### Exam calendar

In fall 2025 classes ran from 1 September to 19 December, with official exam weeks from 20 to 24 October and 15 to 19 December.

| Date (2025) | What |
|---|---|
| 3 to 9 October | Chuseok, the Korean harvest holiday, plus two other public holidays, so no classes (see Travel) |
| 21 and 22 October | Midterms in the Brain course (weight moved to the final) and Database |
| 27 October and 1 December | CS492(C) in-class tests |
| 1 to 12 December | Course evaluations, needed to see your grades on time |
| 8 to 11 December | CS492(C) online poster sessions (mandatory) and CS454 team presentations |
| 16 and 17 December | Finals in the Brain course, Social Computing and Database |
| 18 to 19 December | CS454 reports due |

Exam times and deadlines can move at short notice, so check [KAIST's academic calendar](https://www.kaist.ac.kr/en/html/edu/03100101.html) for your year and don't plan anything tight around the exam weeks.

### Course platforms

KLMS (KAIST Learning Management System, at [klms.kaist.ac.kr](https://klms.kaist.ac.kr)) holds materials and submissions in many courses, but day to day my diffusion course and CS454 ran on Slack, CS473 on a Google Group and Campuswire (a class discussion app), and the database course on Classum, another class communication app. Join all of them in the first week. For picking courses, [otl.kaist.ac.kr](https://otl.kaist.ac.kr) is a student-built site with timetables and course reviews.

### Transcript and credits

KAIST only sends your transcript if you file the Campus Clearance Form before you leave (see Leaving). In fall 2025 grades appeared on the portal from 25 December for those who'd done the course evaluations, could be corrected until 30 December and were final on 31 December. KAIST also needed a Personal Information Consent Form from every exchange student by 5 January 2026. My transcript arrived by email on 19 January, and two paper copies were posted to my DTU coordinator from the end of January. KAIST's 2027 fact sheet says students can now download a free official English transcript in early January, so it may be quicker for you.

When you're home, apply for the final credit transfer on [merit.dtu.dk](https://merit.dtu.dk). If you took the same courses as on your latest pre-approval, you can base the application on it, and if not, you start a new one. On an exchange agreement like KAIST's, the transcript usually goes straight to DTU, which adds it to your case and emails you, so DTU asks you not to wait for it before applying. Processing takes up to 8 weeks.

Everything you pass transfers as one block marked "passed", with its ECTS, and the letter grades never show up on your DTU transcript or diploma or count in your grade average. My five courses came to 27.5 ECTS, and a three-week January course at DTU brought my semester to 32.5 (see Applying for the conversion rules and the master's rate).

### The DTU travel report

DTU also wants a travel report in English, written in your Beyond Borders profile under "My Overview", as one of the conditions in your selection letter. Past KAIST reports are only visible to logged-in DTU users. I handed mine in and found it "a waste of time, I don't think anyone has gotten any use from it." I hope people get some use from this guide.
