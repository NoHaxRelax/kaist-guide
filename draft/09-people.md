## People

### The exchange crowd

There were about 200 exchange students in my fall 2025 cohort. Around 8 of us were Danes, all men as it happened. We went out to restaurants together regularly, some of us went to Jeju together, and I still see one of them today. We do hackathons together and play padel.

Almost all exchange students live on campus, most of them in the W6 dorm (see Campus life), and past exchange reports say this is a big reason friendships form so fast.

### The buddy programme

ISSS runs a buddy programme for undergraduate exchange students. You sign up through the same pre-arrival survey that sets up your dorm room (due 18 July in 2025), and you're put in a group with Korean students as your buddies. Mine was a first-year student. The programme starts with a buddy ceremony straight after orientation, and my group's chat ran on WhatsApp and Instagram.

In fall 2025 the programme ran these events.

| Event | Date (2025) | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Buddy ceremony | 29 August | Right after orientation, in the main auditorium |
| Day trip to Gongju, a historic city nearby | 27 September | 20,000 KRW including lunch, 75 places given out by lottery |
| Halloween party | 28 October | On campus |
| Kimbap and pepero workshop | 13 November | Making Korean rice rolls and chocolate-dipped biscuit sticks, 25 places |

My group was quite shy, so I went looking for my own people, the outgoing, social, fun and smart ones. For me the buddy programme was a waste of time, but it varies a lot from group to group. My advice is to say yes by default. Give it a couple of tries, and if it doesn't click, quietly stop going. Whatever the emails say, there are no real consequences.

### First friends

I met my first friends, two or three Romanians, in the queue for the machine where you get your student status confirmed (see Arrival and first weeks). They turned out to be in some of my classes, so we ended up doing group work together over WhatsApp.

A Kazakh friend introduced me to his Kazakh friends. They invited us on hikes and to what felt like every party in Daejeon, and one of them had a car and knew where the good spas were. At the end of the semester my Kazakh friend thanked us "for not judging". It surprised all of us Europeans, and it moved me.

Everyone is as nervous as you are in the first days, so keep trying and you'll find your group. I never felt lonely, because there was always someone to talk to. My best advice is to meet as many different people as possible.

Among the international students, plans got made on WhatsApp. In 2025 there was a WhatsApp group for the whole exchange cohort, and the buddy groups and my course groups used it too. KakaoTalk is the main messaging app in Korea, so it's worth having as well.

### Korean students

To be honest, I didn't make Korean friends. The one exception was a friend we had in common through DTU, who had lived in Europe for years.

Koreans are very polite and friendly when you approach them, but they rarely start a conversation themselves. My impression, and I don't want to generalise about a whole country, is that many Korean students understand far more English than they speak and would sooner stay quiet than sound stupid. From what I saw, school there is very memory- and test-based, and as I see it, "to get good at speaking English you have to speak English, and sound stupid for a couple of years."

A May 2024 survey by the KAIST Herald, a campus newspaper, found that 48.1% of Korean KAIST students had no contact with international students, and both groups named language as the main barrier. So if you want Korean friends, you'll have to make the first move. Clubs are another way in. Some clubs don't accept foreigners and some hold auditions, but ISSS hands out a list of international-friendly clubs (according to reports from 2023 to 2026).

### Dating

Dating locally is fine, and my only rule is "don't be a dick about it." Know the dorm rules before you bring anyone home, because the dorms are split by gender and an overnight guest of the other gender can get you thrown out of the dorm for good (see Campus life).

### Visitors from home

A friend from DTU who was on exchange in Japan came to visit me for a couple of weeks, and my fiancée visited too.

Danes can visit Korea without a visa for up to 90 days, and they are exempt from K-ETA (Korea Electronic Travel Authorization, an online travel permit) until 31 December 2026, so check the rule again if someone is visiting after that. Unannounced guests in the dorm cost 50 penalty points (see Campus life), so sort out where your visitors will sleep before they arrive.
